# M2: North Star and Guardrail Metrics

About 10 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, three levels of metrics for AI features, and why acceptance can lie. Today, how teams decide which number to chase, and how to stop that chase from hurting users. This is one of the most effective places for a designer to influence a metric driven team.

## The bottom line

A north star metric is the one number a team tries to move, because it best reflects real value delivered to users. Guardrail metrics are numbers that must not get worse while you chase it. Leading indicators move early and predict the north star. Lagging indicators move late and confirm it. For AI features, good guardrails come straight from knowledge designers hold: where users get confused, annoyed, or hurt.

## North star

A good north star reflects value delivered, not activity.

Messages generated by the assistant is activity. The assistant can generate more messages by being worse, because users have to ask again. Tasks completed with the assistant's help is much closer to value.

A useful 2026 framing from writing on AI A B testing separates outcome metrics, whether the user got what they were trying to get, from activity counts like tokens or suggestions shown. For a code completion product, the outcome is the accepted edit that survives, not the suggestion displayed. For a search product, it is the satisfied query, not the click.

A good north star also needs to be something the team can influence, and that, when it rises, the business is likely to benefit too.

## Why one number can mislead

Any metric that becomes a target can be pushed in harmful ways. This idea is often called Goodhart's law, after the economist Charles Goodhart: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

If the target is assistant usage, a team might make the assistant appear more often, interrupt more, and offer suggestions everywhere. Usage rises. Users grow annoyed. Eventually they turn it off, but that shows up months later.

That is what guardrails are for.

## Guardrails

Guardrails are metrics that protect what matters while you chase the north star. The rule is simple: the north star may go up only if the guardrails hold.

For AI features, common guardrails include:

Correction and undo rates on assistant actions.

Negative feedback and complaint rates.

Toggle off and notification opt out rates.

Latency, especially the slowest responses, not just the average.

Cost per completed task, since AI features have real per use costs.

And error rates on high impact actions.

Take a moment. In your own words, why does every north star need guardrails, and why do harms often show up late?

## From metrics to rules

A 2026 trend makes guardrails more concrete. Some teams turn them into automatic policies with thresholds and actions. For example: if the rate of outputs that break the required format drops below a set level, switch the feature to a simpler, safer mode. If the undo rate on an action passes a threshold, require confirmation for it.

This connects directly to the checks in S6. Rules that run automatically are guardrails with teeth.

## Leading and lagging

Lagging indicators confirm results, but slowly. Retention after ninety days. Revenue. Brand trust surveys.

Leading indicators move sooner, and predict them. Completion of a first successful task. Use in the first week. Whether a user returns after their first failure.

Teams watch leading indicators to steer, and lagging ones to confirm they steered right. For AI features, a particularly useful leading indicator is recovery after failure: do users keep using the feature after it gets something wrong? That predicts long term trust better than satisfaction scores.

## Worked example one: a designer proposes guardrails

A team sets its north star: weekly tasks completed with the assistant.

A designer proposes three guardrails. Undo rate on assistant actions, because the team is about to expand what the assistant can change. Dismissal rate on unrequested suggestions, because behavioral semantics from S5 say unrequested content needs a high bar. And time to complete the same tasks manually, to make sure the standard path does not quietly get worse as investment shifts to the assistant.

Leading indicator: users who complete one successful task in their first week.

Two months later, the north star rises. Dismissal rate rises too. The guardrail triggers a review. The team finds the assistant has started offering suggestions on nearly every screen. They reduce unrequested suggestions before users start turning the feature off.

Without the guardrail, the dashboard would have shown pure success.

## Worked example two: where guardrails fail

Another team has guardrails, but they are vague. Maintain user satisfaction. No number, no threshold, no owner.

When the north star rises and satisfaction surveys dip slightly, nobody acts. Was the dip significant? Who decides? The guardrail exists on paper only.

Guardrails need three things to work: a specific metric, a threshold, and an agreed action when it is crossed. Otherwise they are good intentions.

There is also the opposite failure: too many guardrails. Twenty protected metrics, and every experiment trips one by chance. The team stops trusting them. A few well chosen guardrails, tied to real risks, work better than a long list.

## Where design fits

Designers often hold the knowledge that makes guardrails good. You know where users get annoyed, confused or harmed, because you have watched them in research. That knowledge can become a guardrail before it becomes a problem.

And proposing a guardrail is one of the most effective ways for a designer to influence a metric driven team. It speaks their language, it protects users, and it shows you think about the business, not just the interface.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against heavy focus on north stars: one number oversimplifies. Real products serve many needs, and forcing a single metric can push teams to neglect everything it does not capture. Some teams prefer a small balanced set instead.

That is fair. But guardrails are exactly the answer to that worry. A north star with good guardrails is a balanced set, with a clear priority. What matters is that the team knows what it is chasing and what it is protecting.

## Practice scenario

Your team wants to make the assistant more proactive: suggesting actions before users ask. The north star is weekly assisted tasks.

Your question. What three guardrails would you propose, with what thresholds? And what leading indicator would tell you early that proactivity is helping, not annoying?

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Choose a north star that reflects value delivered, not activity.

Two. Protect it with a few specific guardrails, each with a threshold, an owner, and an agreed action.

Three. Designers can lead on guardrails. It turns user insight into team language.
