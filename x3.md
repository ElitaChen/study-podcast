# X3: The Kernel of Strategy, and the Crux

About 12 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, framing a problem with situation, complication and question.

Today, strategy. A word used so often it has almost lost its meaning. Every roadmap, vision deck and planning document calls itself a strategy. We will use one clear structure to tell real strategy from its imitations, and then apply it at your scale, to a team and to your own work.

## The bottom line

A real strategy has three parts, which Richard Rumelt calls the kernel. A diagnosis of what is really going on. A guiding policy: the overall approach that deals with it, and that rules things out. And coherent actions that carry out the policy and reinforce each other. In his later book, Rumelt adds the crux: the one part of the challenge that is both critical and solvable, where effort will actually pay off.

## Where this comes from

Richard Rumelt is a professor emeritus at UCLA. His book Good Strategy Bad Strategy, from 2011, became one of the most cited books on the topic. In 2022 he published The Crux, which sharpens the first part of the kernel.

His central claim is blunt. Most organizations do not have a strategy. They have goals, slogans, and long lists of initiatives, and they call that strategy.

## Part one: diagnosis

The diagnosis names the critical challenge. Not every problem. The one that matters most, and why it is hard.

A good diagnosis simplifies. Reality is overwhelming: dozens of problems, competing priorities, noise. A diagnosis says: of all this, these one or two things are what really matter.

Example: our AI features feel inconsistent, because each team defines behavior in its own way, and nothing shared tells generated output what is allowed.

Notice this explains why, not just what. Inconsistent AI features is a symptom. Each team defines behavior separately, with nothing shared, is a diagnosis.

## The crux

In his 2022 book, Rumelt sharpens this. A diagnosis can still be broad. The crux is narrower: the pivotal part of the challenge that is both critical and addressable. Not just what the problem is, but what its linchpin is.

The test for a crux has two sides. If you solved it, would much of the larger problem give way? And can you actually solve it with the resources you have?

In the example, the crux might be: there is no shared, machine readable description of what our components mean and when they may be used. Solve that, and many teams can align without being forced to. And it is addressable, starting small.

Take a moment. In your own words, what is the difference between a diagnosis and a crux?

## Part two: guiding policy

The guiding policy is the overall approach chosen to deal with the diagnosis. It is not a list of tasks. It is a direction that rules some options in and others out.

Example: make the design system the single source of behavior for AI features, readable by both people and models.

The ruling out is what gives it force. This policy rules out each team writing its own style prompts. It rules out fixing inconsistency screen by screen after launch. A guiding policy that rules nothing out is not a policy. It is a hope.

## Part three: coherent actions

Actions that carry out the policy and reinforce each other.

Write component contracts for the ten most used components. Add semantic properties like destructive and needs confirmation. Run one feature end to end with generated UI using only those components. Add automatic checks for the rules.

Each action makes the others more valuable. The contracts are useless without a feature that uses them. The feature is risky without the checks. The checks need the semantic properties to check against. That interdependence is what coherent means. The opposite is a list of good initiatives that merely coexist in the same document, each pulling in its own direction.

## Proximate objectives

Rumelt adds one more idea that is very practical. Proximate objectives: near term targets close enough that the team can actually hit them.

Be the leader in AI experience is not proximate. Ship one feature where generated UI uses only contracted components, by the end of next quarter, is. A good leader, Rumelt argues, absorbs the ambiguity of the big picture and hands the team an objective they can achieve.

## Spotting bad strategy

Rumelt describes common signs of bad strategy.

Goals presented as strategy. Become the leader in AI experience. A wish, not a plan.

Fluff. Big, abstract language that hides the absence of hard choices.

Failure to face the challenge. A plan that never says what is difficult.

And a list of unrelated actions, often called strategic priorities, that pull in different directions.

The fastest test: ask what the diagnosis is. If nobody can say what the core challenge is, it is not a strategy, however polished the document.

## Worked example one: your own learning

Apply the kernel to your own growth.

Diagnosis: everyone on the team now builds working prototypes with AI. Prototyping alone no longer sets anyone apart.

Crux: the team has no one who defines what AI features should do and mean, in a form engineers and models can act on.

Guiding policy: be the person who defines AI behavior, through specs, semantics and decisions, within your own scope. This rules out competing on prototype speed, and rules out reaching into other designers' work.

Coherent actions: the spec format from the P group, applied to your own features. The semantic layer and component contracts. The spectrum map essay as a visible artifact. A small pilot shown one on one to your PM and tech lead.

Proximate objective: one feature, fully specified in the new format, shipped next quarter.

## Worked example two: where the kernel misleads

A team writes a perfect kernel. Clear diagnosis, sharp policy, coherent actions. Six months later, nothing has changed.

What happened? The actions required capabilities the team did not have, or time that was never freed up. The kernel looked coherent on paper, but was incoherent with reality.

Rumelt would say that strategy includes the actions, so if the actions cannot be done, the strategy is broken. But in practice, people write the kernel and treat execution as someone else's problem.

The lesson: check feasibility as part of coherence. For each action, who does it, and what do they stop doing to make room?

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against the kernel: it assumes you can diagnose the situation clearly. In fast moving fields like AI, the situation changes every few months. A sharp diagnosis today may be wrong by spring. Some argue that in such conditions, experimentation and adaptation beat a fixed strategy.

There is truth in this. But the kernel can include experimentation. The guiding policy can be: learn fast in small slices before committing. And the diagnosis can be revisited each quarter. What the kernel still demands is that you say what you believe the challenge is, so you know what you are testing. Adapting without a diagnosis is just drifting.

## A related framework

You may also hear about Roger Martin's strategy choice cascade: where to play and how to win. The two fit together. One way to put it: Martin specifies the choices a strategy must make, and Rumelt specifies the argument a strategy must be. A cascade without a diagnosis answers questions nobody asked. A kernel without clear choices never commits.

Your design organization announces: our strategy for 2027 is to be AI first in everything we design.

Your question. Using the kernel, what is missing? What diagnosis would you propose, what would the crux be, and what would a guiding policy rule out?

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Start with a diagnosis, then find the crux: critical and solvable.

Two. A guiding policy must rule things out. Actions must reinforce each other and be feasible.

Three. Give teams proximate objectives they can actually hit.
