# M1: Measuring AI Features, and Why Acceptance Lies

About 11 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, decision records. Now a group on business language. Metrics are how PMs and leaders talk about whether something works. Learning their words lets you put design arguments in their terms. And in 2026, AI metrics are changing quickly, in ways that favor designers.

## The bottom line

AI features need metrics at three levels. Usage: do people use it? Quality: is the output actually good? Value: does it help them get something done? The most popular AI metric, acceptance rate, measures a click, not an outcome, and can look healthy while a feature quietly fails. In 2026, practice has shifted toward behavioral signals like edit distance and override rate, which are much closer to what designers care about.

## Why traditional metrics fall short

Classic product metrics were built for software that behaves the same way every time. Daily active users. Session length. Feature activation. Conversion.

For AI features, these mislead. Daily active users tells you how many people opened a screen. It says nothing about whether the AI output on that screen was useful. A user who tries the assistant five times, gets five weak answers, and keeps trying looks more engaged than a user who got a perfect answer once and left satisfied.

Several 2026 analyses make this point. Mixpanel, a product analytics company, published a framework for AI product analytics arguing exactly this: standard engagement metrics count both users the same way, and can even score the frustrated one higher.

## Level one: usage

Still worth measuring, with care.

Adoption: of the people who could use the feature, how many tried it?

Repeat use: do they come back?

Toggle off rate: how many turn the feature off? This one is often overlooked, and it is a strong signal of trust breaking.

## Level two: quality

Here is where AI features differ most.

Acceptance rate: how often do people accept a suggestion? This is the most common AI metric. GitHub Copilot made it famous, reporting that roughly thirty percent of code suggestions were accepted.

But here is the problem. Acceptance measures the click. One 2026 analysis put it plainly: acceptance rate measures a user interface interaction, not an outcome. People accept suggestions because changing them feels like too much work, because they did not read carefully, or because accepting is a keyboard shortcut. And they then rewrite the accepted text a minute later.

Take a moment. In your own words, why can a high acceptance rate hide a quality problem?

## Better quality signals

So practice has moved to signals that look at what happens next.

Edit distance: how much of the accepted output survives into the final version? High acceptance with little editing means the feature produces answers. High acceptance with heavy editing means it produces starting points. That has value, but a different value than the acceptance number implies.

Override rate: how often do users edit, dismiss, or ask again after an AI response? Mixpanel calls this one of the earliest warning signs of quality problems.

Regeneration rate: how often do users throw the output away and ask for a new one? Editing means the output was salvageable. Regenerating means it was not.

Abandonment: how often does someone start a request and give up?

And a useful combination. Low edit rate plus low task completion is a danger signal. It may mean users are not editing because they are abandoning.

## Level three: value

Task success: did the user complete what they came to do?

Time saved: compared to doing it without the feature.

Downstream outcomes: fewer support tickets, more completed setups, better retention of the product overall.

Value is the hardest to measure, and the one that justifies investment.

## A note on benchmarks

You will see numbers like: forty to fifty percent unedited acceptance is solid, above seventy is excellent. Treat these with caution. One 2026 guide for product managers pointed out that public benchmarks for acceptance and edit distance are thin, and advised treating them as internal trend lines, not numbers to compare against other companies. Different features, users and tasks make cross product comparison almost meaningless.

## Worked example one: smart replies

A smart reply feature launches. The PM reports forty percent adoption and thirty five percent acceptance. Good news.

A designer asks for more. Edit distance shows that accepted replies are usually cut by half before sending. Regeneration rate is low. Time saved is modest.

Reading together: the suggestions are directionally right, but too long. Users accept and trim.

Design response: change the default length, and update the golden examples in the spec to shorter replies. Next month, edit distance drops, and time saved rises.

Same feature. The second set of metrics told the team what to fix.

## Worked example two: where metrics mislead in another direction

An assistant that summarizes notifications shows very low edit rates and high acceptance. Looks excellent.

But summaries are not usually edited. People read them and act. The right question is whether they acted correctly. A diary study, from U1, reveals that users sometimes miss important items that the summary left out.

The lesson: for outputs that are read rather than edited, edit distance tells you little. You need outcome signals, like whether users later opened the full notifications for items the summary skipped, or research.

Every metric fits some feature shapes and not others. Choosing metrics is a design decision about what good means for this feature.

## Linking metrics to design

Here is where metrics meet the rest of this series.

Override and correction rates measure the correction paths from P4.

Abandonment often points to the working state from P2: people leave while waiting.

Edit distance shows whether the description and golden examples in the spec match what users want.

Toggle off rate is a trust signal, connected to B5 and U2.

So metrics are not only for PMs. They are evidence for design decisions, and designers who propose the right metrics gain a strong voice in metric driven teams.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against behavioral metrics: they are hard to instrument. Edit distance needs the original output and the final version, at the right moment. Override needs clear definitions. Many teams lack the data pipeline, and acceptance is simply what they can measure.

That is a real constraint. The practical answer: start with one better signal for your most important feature, not all of them. And when acceptance is all you have, report it with an honest caveat, so nobody mistakes it for success.

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Measure usage, quality and value. Usage alone misleads for AI.

Two. Acceptance measures a click. Pair it with edit distance, override and regeneration.

Three. Choose metrics that fit the feature's shape, and treat outside benchmarks as rough at best.
