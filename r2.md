# R2: Scaling Laws, and Whether They Still Hold

About 11 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, reasoning models and test time compute. Spending more at the moment of use, with diminishing returns and even overthinking.

Today, the older idea underneath the whole field. Spending more at training time. It explains why AI improved so fast, why companies are spending hundreds of billions on data centers, and why people now argue about whether that era is ending.

## The bottom line

Scaling laws say that as you increase model size, data, and training compute together, a model's prediction error falls in a smooth and predictable way. That predictability turned AI progress into something companies could plan and invest in. Today there is a real debate about whether the pattern still holds, mainly because high quality data is running out. Both sides have evidence.

## What a scaling law is

In 2020, a team at OpenAI led by Jared Kaplan published a paper that changed the field. They trained many models of different sizes, on different amounts of data, with different amounts of compute. Then they plotted the error against each of these.

On a special kind of chart, called a log log plot, where each step on the axis means multiplying by ten, the points fell almost on a straight line.

That straight line was the discovery. It meant: if you multiply compute by ten, you can predict roughly how much better the model will get, before you build it.

This is called a power law. The important feature of a power law is that each improvement costs more than the last. To get the next equal step of improvement, you need to multiply resources again. Diminishing returns are built into the math.

## Why this mattered so much

Before scaling laws, building a bigger model was a gamble. After them, it was closer to engineering planning. A company could say: with this much compute, we expect roughly this capability. That made it possible to justify enormous investment.

Most of the current race, the giant data centers, the chip shortages, the energy deals, rests on this one empirical pattern.

## Chinchilla: size is not the only lever

In 2022, a team at DeepMind refined the picture. Their paper, usually called the Chinchilla paper, after the model they trained, showed that many large models had been trained wrong. They were too big for the amount of data they saw.

Their finding: for a fixed compute budget, model size and training data should grow together. Roughly twenty tokens of training text for each parameter of the model. A smaller model trained on much more data beat a larger model trained on less.

That changed how models were built. But practice has moved further since. Chinchilla only optimized for training cost. In the real world, a model is trained once but used billions of times. A smaller model is cheaper to run every single time someone uses it. So companies now often train models far past the Chinchilla ratio, on hundreds of tokens per parameter, accepting higher training cost in exchange for a smaller, cheaper model to serve.

The lesson is useful beyond AI. The best design depends on what you are optimizing for. Optimize for the one time cost, and you get one answer. Optimize for the lifetime cost, and you get a different one.

## The data wall

Here is the pressure point. Scaling laws assume an endless supply of high quality training data. There is not.

Epoch AI, a research group that tracks these trends, has projected that the stock of high quality human written text could be effectively used up somewhere between 2026 and 2030, depending on how efficiently it is used. Different analysts give different dates, but the range keeps landing around now.

What happens then? Several options are being tried. Synthetic data, meaning text generated by models to train other models, with mixed results so far. Repeating data more carefully. Using more kinds of data, like video, which is part of why world models from season two matter. And shifting effort to other levers entirely, like the test time compute from the last episode, and reinforcement learning after pretraining.

One recent analysis, published by Dwarkesh Patel and Jerry Han in September 2026, looked at six years of pretraining progress and argued that most of it came from better data, not better model designs. If that is right, the data question is even more central than it looked.

## The debate, fairly

Now the honest part. People disagree about whether scaling is slowing.

The slowing side says: pretraining gains have become harder to get, data is running out, each ten times more compute buys less, and the industry pivoted to reasoning models partly because pure pretraining stalled.

The holding side says: new frontier models keep showing strong gains. When Google released Gemini 3 in late 2025, some investors and analysts argued it proved pretraining scaling still works, and that the wall was a mirage.

The fair summary: the original curve still describes what happens when you add more compute and data. What is uncertain is whether enough new data and compute will be available to keep following it, and how much of future progress will come from other levers instead.

## Emergence, briefly

One more contested idea. As models grew, some abilities seemed to appear suddenly. Small models could not do a task at all, then above some size they could. This was called emergence.

In 2023, a team at Stanford argued that many such jumps come from how tasks are scored. If you only give credit for a fully correct answer, steady improvement looks like a sudden leap. With smoother scoring, the curve looks gradual. Today, most researchers accept that abilities improve with scale, sometimes quickly, but that how sudden it looks depends partly on measurement.

## Worked example one: planning with a moving target

Your team designs an assistant feature that summarizes long documents. Today, the model misses subtle points in about one in five long documents. Should the team ship?

Scaling thinking suggests the model underneath will likely improve within a year or two. So design the feature to upgrade gracefully. Build the eval set from P5 now, with golden, edge, and negative examples. When a new model arrives, run the eval. If it clears the bar, switch. No redesign needed. The design work and the evals survive the model change.

## Worked example two: where the scaling story misleads

Another team waits. Their plan: the feature is not good enough yet, but models will get better, so we will ship when they do.

This is where scaling thinking misleads. Not every weakness is a model weakness. If the feature fails because retrieval finds the wrong documents, or because the spec never defined what a good summary is, a better model will not fix it. Scaling improves prediction. It does not fix a missing design.

So before waiting for a better model, ask: is this failure about the model, or about the product layer around it? A3's five part map helps here.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against caring about scaling as a designer: it is an industry level story, about investment and research, and it changes little about the daily work of designing a feature.

That is partly fair. You do not need to predict model progress. But it matters in two practical ways. It tells you to design for model upgrades, with evals ready. And it helps you push back in planning when a team assumes the model will fix a problem that is really a design problem.

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Progress from scale has been unusually predictable, but each step costs more than the last.

Two. Data may be the binding limit now, which is why other levers like reasoning and new data types matter.

Three. Design for model upgrades with evals, and never assume a better model will fix a design problem.
