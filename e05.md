# S5.5: Events and Background Work

About 11 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, speed: caching, prefetching and optimistic interfaces. So far, everything has happened while the user is looking at the app. Today, what happens when they are not. Notifications, triggers, scheduled jobs. Much of what makes an app feel alive, or annoying, happens in the background.

## The bottom line

Apps act in the background in three ways: the server sends an event to the device, the device runs work on a schedule, or something in the world triggers an action. On phones, the operating system tightly limits background work, to protect battery and privacy. So background features are always a negotiation between what the app wants and what the system allows. Designers need to know these limits, because they decide which experiences are possible.

## Events: something happened

An event is a record that something happened. A message arrived. A meeting moved. A payment cleared. A package left the warehouse.

Most modern systems are built around events. When something happens on the server, it creates an event, and other parts of the system react. One reaction might be: tell the user's phone.

## Push: the server taps the device

The phone cannot keep asking the server, has anything changed, every few seconds. That would drain the battery. Instead, Apple runs a central service, the Apple Push Notification service. The app's server sends a message to Apple, and Apple delivers it to the device.

That delivery can do two things. Show a visible notification. Or quietly wake the app for a short moment, so it can fetch new data in the background, without showing anything.

But here is the important limit. Delivery is not guaranteed to be immediate, and quiet background pushes are treated as low priority. The system may delay them, combine them, or skip them, depending on battery, network and how often the user opens the app.

So a design that assumes the app will always update silently in the background, on time, will sometimes fail.

## Scheduled work: the device decides when

Apps can also ask the system to run work later: refresh content, clean up storage, upload photos. The app does not choose the exact moment. It asks, and the system decides when it is a good time, often when the device is charging and on Wi Fi.

For design, this means some promises are impossible. Your library will be fully backed up at midnight cannot be promised. Your library backs up when your phone is charging and connected can.

Take a moment. In your own words, why does the operating system, not the app, decide when background work runs?

## Triggers: the world changes

A third kind of background behavior comes from triggers in the world. Arriving at a place. Connecting to a car. A focus mode turning on. A time of day.

These are powerful for context aware features. But they rely on permissions, which episode six covers, and on the system deciding to deliver them. Location triggers in particular are limited to protect privacy and battery.

## Live, continuous updates

Some experiences need to update for minutes or hours: a delivery on its way, a sports score, a ride arriving. Apple's Live Activities are designed for this. They show on the lock screen and in the Dynamic Island, and are often updated by pushes from the server.

These are a good example of system design shaping experience. The update frequency is limited. The content must fit tight layouts. And the activity has a lifespan. A designer who knows these limits designs a live experience that degrades gracefully, for example by showing an estimated time that stays useful even if an update is late.

## The design of interruption

Background work often ends in an interruption: a notification, a sound, a banner. This connects directly to S5, behavioral semantics. iOS has four interruption levels: passive, active, time sensitive, and critical.

The system design view adds one more insight. Every notification is the visible end of a long chain: an event on a server, a decision to notify, a push through Apple, a decision by the device about how to show it given focus settings. A designer who sees the whole chain can ask better questions. Which events deserve a notification at all? Who decides urgency, and based on what? What happens if the same event is notified on three devices at once?

## Worked example one: a meeting change

A meeting organizer moves your three o'clock to two thirty.

Chain: the calendar server records the change, an event. A rule decides whether to notify, perhaps only if the meeting is within the next day. A push goes to all your devices. Each device decides how to show it, based on focus mode and urgency.

Design decisions along the chain. Notify only if the change affects the next twenty four hours. Mark it time sensitive if the new time is within an hour. Show it on one device, ideally the one in use, and mark it as seen everywhere once read on any device.

That last decision, deduplication across devices, is often missed. Without it, the same alert buzzes on a phone, a watch and a laptop at once.

## Worked example two: where background assumptions fail

A team designs a feature: your daily summary will be ready when you wake up, generated overnight.

They assume the app will run work at five in the morning. In testing, it works. In real use, many users find no summary, or yesterday's. The system delayed the background work, because the phone was not charging, or the app had not been used recently.

Better design: generate the summary on the server, where timing is reliable, and deliver it with a push. Or generate it on the device the moment the user opens the app, with a short, well designed loading state. Either way, never promise timing the system does not guarantee.

## Going deeper: events and AI agents

Background work matters more as agents arrive. An agent asked to watch for flight price drops, or to follow up if nobody replies by Friday, is a background feature by definition.

The same limits apply, plus new questions. Where does the agent run while the user is away? Usually on a server, because phones cannot run long background jobs. What does it do when it finds something? Notify, or act? And how does the user see what it did while they were not looking?

This is P2 and P4 again, at system level. Background agents need a clear record of actions, and the impact grid from P3 to decide what they may do alone.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against designers learning these limits: they change with every operating system release. Designers will always be a step behind. Better to describe the ideal experience and let engineers say what is possible.

Partly right. Specific limits change. But the shape stays stable: the system controls background timing, delivery is not guaranteed, and battery and privacy come first. Designing with that shape in mind avoids promises that cannot be kept, and saves whole rounds of rework.

## Connections

This connects to S5, interruption levels. To episode one, the server as the reliable place for timed work. To P3 and P4, for background agents. And to cross platform design, through deduplication across devices.

## Practice scenario

Your team wants an assistant feature that reminds people to leave for meetings based on traffic.

Your question. Trace the chain: what event starts it, where is the decision made, how does it reach the device, and how is urgency decided? Which part depends on background timing that might not be reliable?

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Background behavior comes from server events, system scheduled work, and triggers in the world.

Two. The operating system controls timing. Never promise exact background timing on a device.

Three. Every notification is the end of a chain. Design the whole chain, including deduplication across devices.
