# S5.3: State and Sync, Offline and Many Devices

About 12 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, data models: entities, properties, relationships, and why a missing field can make a design impossible. Today we put those objects in motion. The same object, on several devices, changed in different places, sometimes without a network. This is sync, and it is where cross platform experiences are won or lost.

## The bottom line

When the same data lives on several devices, copies will sometimes disagree. Sync is how they come back together. The system can make a change wait for the server, merge changes automatically, or ask a person to choose. Each approach creates a different experience. And a mindset shift from 2026 practice is useful for designers: conflicts are not just failures, they are information about what people were trying to do.

## Why copies disagree

Picture three copies of your notes: on your iPhone, your Mac, and the server. You edit on the phone in the subway. Your Mac is asleep. The phone cannot reach the server.

For a while, three copies hold three slightly different truths. Nothing has gone wrong yet. This is normal for any app that works offline or on several devices.

The question is what happens when they reconnect.

## Three ways to bring copies back together

One. Make the change wait. The device refuses to change anything until it reaches the server. Simple, always consistent, but useless offline, and slow on bad networks.

Two. Last write wins. When two changes collide, the most recent one is kept, and the other disappears. Simple, and often fine for small things like a setting. But if two people edited the same note, one person's work silently vanishes. Silent loss is the worst kind of failure, because nobody knows to look for it.

Three. Merge. The system combines both changes. If one person changed the title and another changed the body, keep both. If both added items to a list, keep all items. This is what modern sync engines aim for.

## CRDTs, without the math

You will hear the term CRDT, short for conflict free replicated data type. You do not need the math. The idea is enough.

A CRDT is a way of storing data so that copies on different devices can be changed independently and later merged, always reaching the same result, no matter what order the changes arrive in. One 2026 guide described it simply: a data structure designed so multiple copies can be modified independently and merged without conflicts.

This is what powers collaborative editing in many modern tools, and the local first apps from episode one.

But notice a subtle point. Conflict free means the system always produces a result. It does not mean the result is what people wanted. If two people rename the same section differently, a CRDT will deterministically pick one or combine them. Mathematically consistent. Possibly still confusing to the humans involved.

Take a moment. In your own words, what does a CRDT guarantee, and what does it not guarantee?

## Conflicts are information

A useful idea comes from the company Ditto, which builds sync technology. In a 2025 article about offline first apps, they argued that conflicts are not failures, they are information, and that teams should shift from preventing concurrency to designing for it.

For a designer, this reframing is powerful. If two family members both checked off milk, that is information: two people were shopping at once. If two colleagues edited the same paragraph, that is information: the paragraph matters to both of them, and maybe they should talk.

So the design question changes. Not only: how do we hide conflicts? But also: when should people see that a conflict happened, and what would help them?

## When to use which

The 2026 overviews of local first sync are refreshingly honest about cost. They point out that most apps do not need full CRDT based sync. What most apps need is to feel fast, and a simpler technique, updating the screen immediately and confirming with the server in the background, gives most of the benefit. That is episode four.

Full local first sync is worth it for apps used in places without reliable connections, apps where many people edit the same thing at once, and single user apps that sync across many devices, like notes, todo lists and journals.

So a designer's question in planning is: how often will people truly use this offline, or together at the same moment? The answer guides how much sync machinery the team should build.

## Sync status is a design surface

Whatever approach is chosen, sync adds states to the feature, which belong in the P2 state list.

Saved on this device, not yet synced.

Syncing.

Synced everywhere.

Could not sync, with a reason.

Conflict resolved automatically, maybe worth a quiet note.

Conflict needs your choice.

Most apps show almost none of these, and that is often right. Constant sync indicators create anxiety. But at important moments, people need to know. Before closing a laptop. Before handing a device to someone. When sharing something that might not be up to date.

## Worked example one: the family shopping list, redesigned

Back to the shopping list from episode one.

Requirement: family members can check items off in a store with no signal, and changes merge when they reconnect.

Merge rules: adding items, always keep both. Checking off, if anyone checked it, it is checked. Editing the same item's text, keep the latest, but show a small note: Ana also edited this.

Sync status: a subtle indicator only when the phone has been offline for a while. Showing changes from 10 minutes ago, will update when connected.

Conflicts as information: if two people check off the same item within a minute, show a gentle note. Ana already got milk. That turns a conflict into coordination.

## Worked example two: where automatic merging misleads

A shared document tool merges everything automatically. Two colleagues, offline on a plane, both rewrite the same introduction paragraph. When they land, the system merges character by character. The result is grammatically broken, mixing sentences from both.

Technically conflict free. Practically useless.

For text where meaning matters, merging at the character level can produce nonsense. Many tools therefore merge automatically when changes touch different parts, but when two people rewrite the same passage, they keep both versions and let a person choose. The design work is that choice moment: showing both versions side by side, clearly, without blame.

## Going deeper: time is not reliable

One surprising fact. Devices do not agree on what time it is. Clocks drift. A phone may be a few seconds or minutes off. So last write wins based on device clocks can pick the wrong winner.

Sync engines solve this with special logical clocks that track the order of events rather than trusting wall clock time. You do not need the details. But it explains a design rule: never promise users that the latest change wins, based on what time they think they made it. Instead, show what happened, and make it easy to restore an earlier version.

That is also why version history is such a valuable feature in synced apps. It is the safety net under every merge rule.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against designers engaging with sync: it is deep technical territory, and users should never have to think about it. The best sync is invisible.

Mostly true. The goal is invisible sync. But invisible sync is designed, not accidental. Someone has to decide merge rules, what to show when it fails, and when a conflict is worth surfacing. If designers do not, those decisions get made by default, often as last write wins, with silent data loss.

## Practice scenario

Your team designs a feature that lets a user and their partner share a trip itinerary, both editing from phones while traveling.

Your question. What merge rules would you write for adding, removing, and editing items? When should a conflict be shown, and how?

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Copies will disagree. Make it wait, last write wins, or merge, each with a different experience.

Two. Conflict free does not mean conflict meaningless. Some conflicts are information worth showing.

Three. Sync adds states. Show them rarely, but clearly at important moments, and back everything with version history.
