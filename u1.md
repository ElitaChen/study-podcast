# U1: Testing AI Features

About 11 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, north star and guardrail metrics. Metrics tell you what is happening. Research tells you why. Today, why classic usability methods break on AI features, what to use instead, and a live debate in 2026 about one of the most popular methods.

## The bottom line

A classic usability test assumes the product behaves the same for every participant. AI features do not. So research has to account for variation in outputs, follow trust over time, and test failure on purpose. Four methods help: controlling variation, diary studies, Wizard of Oz, and prepared failures. But there is a real tension: simulated outputs let you test design ideas early, while testing only with the real model shows what users will actually experience. Good research plans use both, at different stages.

## Why classic tests mislead

In a classic test, five to eight people try the same screens. You watch where they struggle. The screens are the same for everyone, so differences come from the people.

With an AI feature, each participant may get a different output. One gets a great answer. Another gets a weak one. If the session goes well, was it the design, or a lucky output? You cannot tell.

There are two more differences. AI features change between releases, as models and prompts are updated, so research can go stale within weeks. And trust builds or breaks over days. A single session cannot see that.

A 2026 practical guide for AI product research summarized the core problem well: AI products break three assumptions of traditional research. Outputs vary. Trust is the central experience problem. And prompts replace navigation, so you cannot study a fixed path through screens.

## Method one: control the variation

Before the study, run the same prompts many times, and look at the range of outputs. Choose examples on purpose: a typical one, a strong one, a weak one, maybe one that is subtly wrong.

Then test how people respond to each. You learn how the design holds up across the range, not just on one lucky result.

This connects directly to the edge and negative examples in P5. Your research stimuli and your spec examples can be the same set.

## Method two: diary studies

Give people the feature for one to four weeks. Ask them to note moments of use, surprise, trust and frustration, ideally right after they happen.

This shows things no lab session can. When people stop checking outputs. When one bad result breaks trust. Whether trust recovers. When the feature becomes a habit, or is quietly abandoned.

Diary studies are expensive and slow. They ask a lot of participants. But for AI features, they are often the only way to see trust as it actually develops.

## Method three: Wizard of Oz

Before the AI works well, a person behind the scenes produces the responses, while the participant interacts as if with the system.

This lets you test the experience, the states, the confirmations and the tone, before engineering is ready. It is especially useful for discovering what good would feel like, and for writing golden examples.

It requires ethical care. Participants should be told how the study worked afterwards.

## The debate: simulated or real outputs?

Here is the live tension. Some 2026 guides on AI product research argue strongly against testing on mocked outputs. Their reasoning: variation is part of the experience. If you test with carefully chosen or human written responses, you are testing a product that does not exist. Users of the real product will meet weaker outputs, odd phrasings, and surprises your simulation never had.

That is a strong point. A Wizard of Oz study can make a feature look much better than it will be.

But the other side is also strong. Early in design, the real model may be far from ready, or not connected yet. Waiting means designing blind. And controlled stimuli let you isolate design questions: does this confirmation pattern work? That is hard to answer when every session gets a random output.

There is also a middle approach in recent research. A 2026 paper on trust analysis used Wizard of Oz simulation deliberately set to different accuracy levels, to study how trust changes as the system gets better or worse. That uses simulation to understand variation, instead of hiding it.

The practical answer: use simulation early to shape the design, controlled real outputs before launch to test robustness, and the real, live model after launch to see actual experience. Each stage answers a different question. The mistake is using only one.

## Method four: prepared failures

Deliberately show participants a wrong result. A wrong date. A misunderstood request. A failed action.

Then watch. Do they notice? How do they correct it? Do they trust the next answer?

This directly tests the correction paths from P4 and the trust tools from B5. Failure is guaranteed in real use. Better to study it on purpose than discover it in support tickets.

## Worked example one: a photo library assistant

A team designs an assistant that reorganizes the user's photo library into suggested albums.

Wizard of Oz early: a researcher manually groups photos to test whether people want suggested albums at all, and what a good album looks like.

Controlled variation before launch: real model outputs, chosen across the range, including one album that mixes two unrelated trips.

Prepared failure: one suggested album includes a photo that does not belong. Do people notice before accepting?

Diary study after launch: two weeks of real use. Do people keep reviewing suggestions, or start accepting blindly? That answer decides how strong the confirmation step must be.

## Worked example two: when a long study shows something a short one cannot

A 2026 paper at the CHI conference, from researchers at York University and collaborators, studied twelve professional UX evaluators working with conversational AI assistants across five sessions, six hours each. The assistants were designed to seem either novice like or expert like.

The title captures a finding no single session could show: one participant described the assistant as becoming a buddy, while still feeling free to disagree with it. The relationship, and the right amount of trust, developed across sessions.

The design lesson the authors draw is about adapting the assistant's apparent expertise to support calibrated collaboration. For you, the method lesson matters as much: some of the most important findings about AI features only appear over time.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against all this: these methods are slow and expensive. Teams ship AI features weekly. By the time a diary study ends, the model has changed twice.

That is real. The answers are proportion and reuse. Run the expensive methods for the highest risk features only. Keep a standing panel of participants who can be re studied quickly after major changes. And reuse research stimuli as eval examples, so the work pays twice.

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Account for variation. Never judge an AI design on a single output.

Two. Simulation and real outputs answer different questions. Use each at the right stage.

Three. Study trust over time, and test failure on purpose.
