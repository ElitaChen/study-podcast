# R1: Reasoning Models, Thinking Before Answering

About 12 minutes.

## Where we left off

This is season four. It goes deeper than earlier seasons, and it fills the gaps they left. We start with a kind of model you use every day, which behaves differently from the simple picture in A1.

In A1, a language model predicts the next token, one after another, and the answer simply comes out. That is still true. But since late 2024, the most capable models add a step before the answer. They think.

## The bottom line

A reasoning model is still a next token predictor. The difference is that it has been trained to write a long chain of thinking before its final answer. It spends more computation at the moment of the question, and in return it does better on problems that need several steps. But more thinking is not always better. Past a certain point, it can make answers worse.

## Why answering straight away fails

Think about how a basic model handles a hard problem. It starts writing the answer immediately. The first few tokens set a direction. Every later token builds on that direction, because attention looks back at what is already written.

So if the first step is wrong, the model tends to continue confidently down the wrong path. It has no natural moment to stop and reconsider. For simple questions this is fine. For a math problem, a plan with constraints, or a bug hidden in code, it often fails.

## What reasoning models do differently

Reasoning models are trained, usually with reinforcement learning, to write out intermediate steps first. They try an approach. Check it. Notice a contradiction. Back up. Try another approach. Only when this process settles do they write the answer.

The key insight is that the thinking is still just tokens. Nothing magical is added to the model. But those thinking tokens become part of the context. When the model writes its final answer, attention can look back at all that work. The model has, in effect, written itself a scratchpad.

How did they learn to do this? During training, the model attempts many problems where the answer can be checked, like math with known solutions, or code with tests. Attempts that reach correct answers are reinforced. Over many rounds, the model discovers that careful step by step thinking, including checking and backtracking, leads to more rewards. Nobody wrote the rules for good reasoning. The model found patterns that work.

## Test time compute

This idea has a name: test time compute. Test time means the moment the model is used, as opposed to training time.

For years, the main way to make models better was to make them bigger and train them longer. That is scaling at training time, which the next episode covers. Reasoning models opened a second lever. Instead of a bigger model, let the same model think longer when it is used.

Researchers have found that for many hard tasks, spending more compute at test time can be more effective than using a larger model. That is a big shift. It means quality is no longer fixed when a model ships. It can be turned up or down per question.

## The cost side

But every thinking token costs something. Time, because the user waits. Money, because tokens are billed. And energy.

The difference can be large. One recent paper points out that generating eight thousand tokens costs sixteen times more than generating five hundred. If most of those tokens add nothing, that is waste at scale.

## When more thinking hurts

Here is where the fresh research gets interesting. A team from Nanjing University presented a paper at ACL 2026, one of the main language research conferences. They studied what happens as you give reasoning models more and more thinking budget.

They found two things. First, returns shrink. The early thinking helps a lot. Later thinking helps less and less. Second, and more surprising, there is overthinking. Past a certain budget, models sometimes reconsider a correct answer and change it to a wrong one. The authors tracked these moments, which they called flip events, where an answer went from correct to incorrect as thinking continued. In their analysis of those cases, about two thirds were genuine overthinking: the model explicitly reconsidered and abandoned a right answer.

This is a very human failure. You answer a test question correctly, have time left, keep staring at it, and talk yourself into the wrong answer.

They also tested a fix. By watching for signs of overthinking in the reasoning and stopping early, they kept about ninety seven percent of peak accuracy while using about sixty percent of the compute.

One caution, using the six questions from W9. This study used open models on math and science benchmarks. Commercial models and everyday tasks may behave differently. But the direction is clear: more thinking has a ceiling, and sometimes a cliff.

## Worked example one: a task that deserves thinking

A user asks the assistant: plan my week so I can finish the spec by Thursday, given all my meetings.

This is a true multi step problem. The model has to read the calendar, estimate how long the remaining spec work takes, find free blocks, notice that Tuesday afternoon is fragmented into thirty minute gaps that are useless for deep work, handle a conflict on Wednesday, and propose a plan. A first guess is likely to miss something.

A reasoning model fits well here. The design questions are about the wait and the result.

The working state, from P2: summary level. Checking your calendar. Estimating time. Each step shown briefly, because this takes several seconds.

The result: the plan, with a one line note on the main trade off. I moved your Wednesday one on one, because it was the only way to get a three hour block.

And optionally, a way to see the reasoning, for those who want to check how it got there.

## Worked example two: where it breaks

Now the user asks: what time is my next meeting?

There is nothing to reason about. A lookup. If the product routes this through deep thinking, the user waits several seconds for something that should be instant. Worse, if the model overthinks, it might second guess which meeting counts as next, and add confusion.

The lesson: effort must match the task. Many products now decide automatically how much to think, or let users choose. A designer should care about that routing decision, because it shapes the feel of the whole product.

## Showing the thinking

Should the product show the reasoning to users? There are good arguments both ways.

For showing it: people can catch a wrong turn early. It can build trust. It makes the system feel less like a black box.

Against showing it: it can be long and noisy. And there is a deeper problem. The shown reasoning is not guaranteed to be the true cause of the answer. Research on reasoning models has found cases where the written reasoning leaves out factors that actually influenced the result. So reasoning that looks careful can create more trust than it deserves.

A common middle path is a short summary of what the model did, with full detail available on request.

## The strongest counterargument

Here is the best case against making reasoning models central to product design. Most everyday product tasks are simple. Lookups, rewrites, short replies, settings changes. For these, a fast standard model is better: quicker, cheaper, and less likely to overthink. Reasoning is a specialist tool, and designing everything around it slows the whole experience down.

That argument is mostly right. The answer is not to choose one. It is to route: fast by default, deep when the task needs it, with a clear design for each.

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Reasoning models buy accuracy on hard problems with time and cost, through test time compute.

Two. More thinking has diminishing returns and can even hurt. Match effort to the task, and route.

Three. Design the waiting, and decide on purpose how much reasoning to show, knowing it can inflate trust.
