# S5.4: Speed, Caching, and Optimistic Interfaces

About 11 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, sync and conflicts. Today, the experience quality that users notice before almost anything else: speed. Not raw engineering speed, but perceived speed. How fast an app feels is partly physics and partly design.

## The bottom line

Apps feel fast in three ways. Keep a nearby copy of data, which is caching. Do work before the user asks, which is prefetching. And show the result before the server confirms it, which is optimistic UI. Each one creates a design obligation: what to show when the copy is stale, when the guess is wrong, or when the server says no.

## Latency, briefly

From episode one: every trip to a server takes time. A few tenths of a second on a good connection, seconds on a weak one, sometimes forever. People notice delays from about a tenth of a second, and start feeling interrupted after about a second.

So the game is to avoid making the user wait for the network whenever possible.

## Caching: a nearby copy

A cache is a saved copy of data, kept close to where it is needed. On the device, or on servers near the user.

When you open your photos and they appear instantly, they come from a cache on your device, not the server.

The catch is freshness. A cache may be out of date. Engineers have a famous saying, often attributed to Phil Karlton: there are only two hard things in computer science, cache invalidation and naming things. Cache invalidation means deciding when a saved copy is too old to trust.

For designers, the question is: what does the user see when the cached copy might be old? Often the best pattern is: show the cached version immediately, then quietly update it when fresh data arrives. This is often called stale while revalidate. It feels instant, and becomes correct soon after.

But it needs care. If prices, balances or availability can change, showing an old value even briefly can mislead. A seat that looks available but is not. A price that changes after you tap.

Take a moment. In your own words, when is showing a cached value harmless, and when is it risky?

## Prefetching: work before the ask

Prefetching means loading things the user is likely to need next. When you open an email, the app may load the next few. When you scroll a feed, it loads ahead.

Done well, the app feels like it reads your mind. Done badly, it wastes battery and data on things nobody opens.

The design input: which next steps are most likely? Designers often know this better than anyone, from research and analytics. Telling engineers the likely next actions is a quiet but powerful contribution to speed.

## Optimistic UI: show it before it is confirmed

This is the most design relevant technique. When the user acts, the interface updates immediately, as if the server already agreed. The request goes to the server in the background. If it succeeds, nothing more happens. If it fails, the interface has to undo its optimism.

You like a post. The heart turns red instantly. Behind the scenes, the like travels to the server.

A 2026 overview of local first software made an important point: most apps do not need full sync machinery. What they need is to feel fast, and optimistic updates give most of the perceived performance benefit with far less infrastructure.

## The obligation: designing the rollback

Optimistic UI makes a promise it might not keep. So the design must say what happens when the server says no.

For low stakes actions, like a like, a quiet reversal with a small message is enough. Could not save your like. Try again.

For higher stakes actions, optimism can be dangerous. If the user sends money and the screen says sent before the server confirms, a failure afterwards is serious. People may have already closed the app, told someone it was sent, or moved on.

This brings back the grid from P3. Impact and reversibility. Low impact, easy to undo: be optimistic. High impact or hard to undo: wait for confirmation, and design the wait well.

## Designing the wait

When waiting is right, the design can still make it feel shorter and safer.

Show immediate acknowledgment. The button changes state the moment it is tapped, so the user knows the tap registered.

Show progress when it will take more than about a second.

Show partial results as they arrive, like the streaming text from AI answers.

And for long waits, let people leave and come back. Your transfer is processing, we will notify you.

Skeleton screens, gray placeholders shaped like the coming content, can help with perceived speed for content loading. But they can also feel slower if they appear for very short loads, creating flicker. A common pattern is to show them only if loading takes longer than a brief threshold.

## Worked example one: a reminder app

Checking off a reminder: optimistic. The checkmark appears instantly. If sync fails, the reminder stays checked locally, and syncs later. Nothing is lost.

Deleting a list with fifty reminders: still optimistic for speed, but with an undo toast for several seconds, and real deletion on the server delayed until the toast disappears. That gives speed and safety together.

Sharing a list with a new person: not optimistic. The invitation must actually reach the server, because another person depends on it. Show a short sending state, then confirmation.

## Worked example two: where optimism creates confusion

A messaging app shows messages as sent instantly. On a weak connection, several fail. The app marks them with a small red icon at the bottom of a long conversation. The user does not scroll down. They believe their messages were delivered.

The rollback existed, but nobody saw it. Optimistic UI is only safe if failures are as visible as the original success. For messages, that might mean a persistent banner, a notification, or keeping failed messages pinned until resolved.

## Going deeper: AI and speed

AI features add a new kind of latency. Model responses take seconds, and reasoning models from R1 can take much longer. The same tools apply, adapted.

Streaming is the AI version of partial results. Showing text as it is generated makes long answers feel faster.

Caching works too. If many people ask the same question, a cached answer can be instant. But it must be fresh, and personal answers cannot be shared.

Optimism is risky for AI actions. Showing that an AI completed an action before it actually did, especially a write action, combines the dangers of optimism with the uncertainty of AI.

And on device models, which episode eight covers, can make some AI features nearly instant, at the cost of capability.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against optimistic UI: it is a form of lying. The interface says something happened before it did. In a world where trust matters, especially with AI, maybe interfaces should always tell the truth about what is confirmed.

It is a fair ethical point. The resolution is proportion. For tiny, reversible actions, the brief gap between shown and confirmed is harmless and makes the product feel responsive. For anything that matters, honesty wins: show the real status. Deciding where that line falls is a design decision, and it belongs in the spec.

## Practice scenario

Your team designs an assistant action: move all my meetings tomorrow back by thirty minutes.

Your question. Should the calendar update optimistically? What does the user see while the changes go to the server, and what happens if two of the five updates fail?

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Caching, prefetching and optimistic UI make apps feel fast. Each needs a design for staleness or failure.

Two. Be optimistic for low impact, reversible actions. Wait and confirm for the rest.

Three. Failures must be as visible as the original success, or optimism becomes deception.
