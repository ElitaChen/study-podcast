# S5.14: System Specs, Not Just Screen Specs

This is also where your positioning becomes concrete. Others on your team build prototypes. A system spec is what makes a feature buildable, consistent across surfaces, and robust when things go wrong.

## The bottom line

A screen spec describes what users see. A system spec describes what the feature is: its capabilities, data, state, events, rules, dependencies and surfaces, plus the AI behavior from season two. It is shorter than it sounds, because most sections are a few lines. And it changes conversations with engineers, because it answers their questions before they ask. The goal is not a big document. It is a complete one.

## Why screen specs are not enough

A screen spec answers: what does it look like? Engineers then have to answer many other questions alone, often under time pressure. Where does the state live? What happens offline? What if two devices disagree? Which surfaces show it? What if a service fails? Who wins when this feature collides with focus mode?

Each unanswered question becomes a decision someone makes by default. Many of those defaults are fine. Some become the experience problems this season described.

## The structure of a system spec

Here is a structure you can use. Each section is short. Many are a few lines.

One. Intent. What the feature is for, and who it serves. From P1.

Two. Capability. The actions and entities it offers. From episode twelve. This is the core of the spec.

Three. Data. New entities or properties needed, and anything missing from today's model. From episode two.

Four. State and sync. Where the truth lives, offline behavior, and merge rules for conflicts. From episodes one and three.

Five. Speed. Response targets for key actions, and which actions are optimistic. From episode four.

Six. Events and background. What happens when the user is not looking, and which notifications exist, with urgency. From episode five.

Seven. Access. Permissions needed, behavior for granted, partial and denied, account or device level settings, and the purpose of each data access. From episode six.

Eight. Contracts and errors. The user facing errors that are possible, and what each should say. From episode seven.

Nine. AI behavior. Routing, fallbacks, and the AI spec from the P group: states, uncertainty, confirmation, correction, examples and checks. From episode eight and season two.

Ten. Interactions and defaults. Shared resources, resolution rules, and every default, including hidden AI defaults. From episodes nine and ten.

Eleven. Failure. Dependencies ranked as core or enhancement, and the experience when each fails. From episode eleven.

Twelve. Surfaces. Which surfaces render the capability, and the constraint that shapes each. From episode twelve.

Thirteen. Loops and measures. The main feedback loops, the north star contribution, and guardrails. From episode thirteen and M2.

And alongside it: decision records for the hard choices, from X4.

Take a moment. Without looking back, how many of the thirteen sections can you name?

## Requirements, not prescriptions

A key principle. Each section states experience requirements, not technical solutions.

Not: use a CRDT. Instead: two people can edit the same list offline, and no edit is silently lost.

Not: cache the balance. Instead: the balance must never be shown without its freshness when it may be out of date.

This keeps the spec in your territory and respects engineering expertise. It also makes the spec durable. Technical choices can change without changing the requirement.

## Keep it light

Thirteen sections sounds heavy. In practice, for a small feature, most sections are one or two lines, and some say not applicable. A small feature's system spec may be one page. A large one, a few pages.

A useful habit: write the spec as questions first. For each section, what do we not know yet? Bring that list to the first engineering conversation. Fill it in together. That turns the spec from a handoff into a shared model of the feature.

## Worked example one: a shared trip itinerary

Intent: let two travelers plan and follow a trip together, from their phones, even with bad connections.

Capability: add item, edit item, reorder, check in. Entities: trip, item, traveler.

Data: items need an added by property, so each traveler sees who added what.

State and sync: local first. Adding always merges. Edits to the same item keep the latest, with a note naming the other editor.

Speed: all edits optimistic. Sharing the trip with a new traveler waits for the server.

Events: notify the other traveler only when an item within the next twenty four hours changes. Time sensitive if within two hours.

Access: location optional, used only to show nearby items. Without it, items stay in list order.

Errors: could not share, the other traveler's account was not found.

AI behavior: optional suggestions for gaps in the plan, on device first. Suggestions never edit the itinerary without a tap.

Interactions: respects focus modes. Assistant created items are labeled.

Failure: maps is an enhancement, hidden if down. Sync is core, shows clear offline status after a while.

Surfaces: widget with the next item, Live Activity on travel days, Siri for what is next.

Loops: accepted suggestions increase suggestion frequency, dismissals reduce it. Guardrail: dismissal rate.

About one page. Every line answers a question engineers would otherwise guess at.

## Worked example two: where a system spec goes wrong

A designer, excited by this approach, writes a twelve page system spec for a small settings change. Engineers skim it, miss the two lines that matter, and quietly stop reading this designer's specs.

The lesson: completeness means answering the questions that matter for this feature, not filling every section. Scale the spec to the feature. Lead with what is new or risky. And use the pyramid from X1: the most important requirement first.

## Going deeper: specs that models can read

A system spec written in a clear, structured way has one more advantage in 2026. AI coding agents and design tools can read it. A spec that names entities, states, errors and rules explicitly gives an agent the context it needs to build closer to intent, and gives checks something to verify against.

That connects this season back to your design system work. An AI readable design system describes components. An AI readable system spec describes features. Together, they are the infrastructure that lets generated code and generated interfaces stay coherent. That is a strong, concrete version of your position.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against system specs: they assume a designer can know all this before building. In practice, much is discovered while building, and specs written early are partly wrong.

True, and that is why the questions first habit matters. The spec starts as a list of unknowns, becomes a shared model during early engineering conversations, and is updated as discoveries happen. Its value is not being right on day one. It is making the important questions visible early, while they are cheap to answer.

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. A system spec describes what a feature is, not just how it looks, in about thirteen short sections.

Two. Write experience requirements, not technical prescriptions. Start with questions, and scale to the feature.

Three. Structured system specs serve engineers, checks and AI agents, and make your positioning concrete.
