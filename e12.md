# S5.12: One Feature, Many Surfaces

About 11 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, failure spreading through dependencies. Today, the topic closest to cross platform design. A feature is no longer a screen inside an app. It can appear in widgets, notifications, the lock screen, Siri, Spotlight, Shortcuts, Control Center, and AI assistants. Designing a feature now means designing a capability that the whole system can use.

## The bottom line

Modern platforms let apps expose their actions and content as structured capabilities, which the system can then show on many surfaces. On Apple platforms, the App Intents framework is the contract for this. It started with Siri and Shortcuts and has grown to widgets, Control Center, Spotlight, Apple Intelligence, and in iOS 26, interactive snippets. For designers, the core work shifts: define the actions and things your feature offers, name them well, and decide how each surface should present them. The capability is the product. Screens are one rendering of it.

## A short history of App Intents

App Intents arrived in iOS 16 as Apple's structured action API for Shortcuts, Siri and Spotlight. iOS 17 extended it to interactive widgets. iOS 18 added Control Center, deeper Spotlight indexing, and made it the contract for Apple Intelligence's actions.

In iOS 26, Apple added interactive snippets. These let an app present a small interactive view, with buttons, text and controls, inside system contexts like Spotlight, Visual Intelligence or Siri's responses. Apple's WWDC 2025 session gave the example of checking a sports score and immediately starting a Live Activity to follow the game.

The direction is clear. Each year, more of a feature's life happens outside the app.

## The two building blocks

App Intents has two core ideas, and both are design ideas.

Actions, called intents. Things the app can do. Create a reminder. Start a workout. Check a delivery.

Entities. Things the app contains. A reminder, a playlist, a document, a contact.

When an app describes its actions and entities clearly, the system can combine them. Siri can say: add this to my groceries list. Spotlight can find a document. A shortcut can chain actions from several apps.

This should sound familiar. It is the data model from episode two and the API contract from episode seven, exposed to the system. And it is the semantic layer from season two: the meaning of the feature, separate from any screen.

Take a moment. In your own words, what are intents and entities, and why are they design concepts?

## One capability, many renderings

Take a single capability: check the status of a delivery.

In the app: a full screen with a map and a timeline.

In a widget: the current status in one line, and an estimated time.

In a Live Activity: a progress indicator on the lock screen, updated over time.

In Siri: a spoken answer, plus an interactive snippet with a button to contact the driver.

In Spotlight: a result when the user types the store's name.

In Shortcuts: an action other apps can use, returning the status as data.

Same capability, same meaning, six renderings. This is S7, one meaning many platforms, applied across surfaces on one device.

## What changes for design

First, design the capability before the screens. Which actions and entities does this feature offer? What do they need, and what do they return? That list is the foundation for every surface.

Second, naming becomes critical. Siri and AI systems match what people say to the names and descriptions you give. A vague name like process item will never be found. Clear names, like track my order, will.

Third, each surface has its own constraints. Widgets update on the system's schedule, not continuously. Live Activities have strict layouts and update limits. Snippets must be small. Voice has no screen at all. The capability must make sense in the most constrained surface, then add richness where space allows.

Fourth, consistency across surfaces is a system property. If the widget says arriving in ten minutes and the app says fifteen, trust breaks. Both must read the same state, from the same source.

## Discoverability is shifting

One 2026 analysis of iOS 26 for app marketers made an interesting point: Apple Intelligence now surfaces app actions in Siri, Spotlight and the Smart Stack, entirely outside App Store search. Well described intents become a second discovery layer.

For product teams, that means capability design is not just engineering hygiene. It decides whether a feature is found at all.

## Worked example one: a habit tracker

Capability list. Actions: log a habit, check today's progress, start a streak reminder. Entities: habit, streak.

Widget: today's progress as rings, with interactive buttons to log each habit, which work without opening the app.

Control Center: a single control to log the most frequent habit.

Siri: log my water, with a spoken confirmation and a snippet showing progress.

Spotlight: search a habit name to see its streak.

Shortcuts: log habit as an action, so people can combine it with other routines.

Design work focused on names, parameters and the most constrained surfaces first. The app screens came last.

## Worked example two: where capabilities collide with the app's model

A note taking app exposes an action: create a note. Simple.

But inside the app, notes must belong to a folder, and the folder screen is always shown first. The action from Siri has no folder. Where does the note go?

The team adds a default folder. Users later find dozens of notes in a folder they never chose, with no idea how they got there.

The lesson: exposing a capability tests the data model. If an action needs context that only the app's screens provide, the model may need a better default, a parameter, or a question. And the result must be traceable: created from Siri, in Inbox.

## Going deeper: capabilities and AI agents

This connects directly to agents. Structured actions and entities are much easier for AI systems to understand and use than arbitrary screens. Tool use from B1, MCP from B3, and App Intents all express the same idea: describe what you can do, in a structured, named, documented way, and an assistant can do it for people.

So a well designed capability layer serves three audiences at once: system surfaces, voice, and AI agents. That makes it one of the highest leverage design artifacts in a modern product.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against designing for every surface: surfaces fragment the product. Each surface gets a thinner version of the feature, users never see the full experience, and the team spends effort on widgets nobody uses.

A fair concern. Not every capability belongs everywhere. The answer is prioritization: expose the few actions people repeat most, on the surfaces where they happen, and keep the full experience in the app. The capability list makes that prioritization explicit.

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. A feature is now a capability rendered on many surfaces. Design the capability first.

Two. Naming, parameters and the most constrained surface come before rich screens.

Three. Consistency across surfaces is a system property, and a capability layer serves the system, voice and AI agents at once.
