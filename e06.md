# S5.6: Permissions, Privacy, and Identity

About 11 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, background work and the long chain behind every notification. Many of those features depend on access: location, contacts, photos, health data. Today, the system that governs access, and the identity underneath it.

## The bottom line

Identity answers who the user is across devices and services. Permissions answer what an app may see and do. On modern platforms, the user, not the app, controls permissions, and can grant partial access or take it back at any time. So every feature that depends on access must be designed for three cases: granted, partly granted, and denied. Asking for access is itself a design moment, and AI features raise the stakes.

## Identity: who is this

Identity starts with an account. The account ties data to a person, across devices. When you sign into a new phone, your notes appear because the account links them.

Two levels are worth separating. The account is the person. The device is one of their tools. Some settings belong to the account, like your name. Some belong to the device, like screen brightness. And some are unclear. Should notification settings sync across devices, or should your watch be quieter than your phone?

That question, account level or device level, comes up constantly in cross platform design. It deserves an explicit answer for every setting.

## Shared identity is hard

Things get harder with shared contexts. A family shares a photo library. A team shares a workspace. A child uses a parent's tablet.

Now every object needs not just an owner, but a set of people who can see it, edit it, or share it further. These are permissions too, between people rather than between app and system.

## Permissions: what the app may access

On iOS, apps must ask before accessing sensitive data: location, camera, microphone, photos, contacts, health, and more. The system shows a standard prompt, and the user decides.

Over the years, Apple has made permissions more granular. Location can be precise or approximate. Photos can be shared fully or only selected ones. Contacts can be limited to a chosen set. The trend is clear: users increasingly grant partial access.

That creates design work. A feature built for full access must still make sense with partial access, and with none.

## Three cases for every feature

Granted. The feature works as designed.

Partly granted. The feature works with less. A photo app with access to only twenty photos should not show an empty library with an error. It should show the twenty, and explain gently how to add more.

Denied. The feature does not work, but the app should still make sense. Explain what the user is missing, in one line, with a path to settings if they change their mind. Never block the whole app because one permission is missing.

## Asking well

A permission prompt can be asked only once in many cases. If the user says no, the app usually cannot show the system prompt again. They have to go to settings. So timing matters enormously.

Ask in context, at the moment the feature needs it, not at first launch. Asking for location when the user taps find nearby makes sense to them. Asking on the welcome screen does not.

Explain the value before the prompt, in plain words, from the user's point of view. Not: we need your location. Instead: to show stores near you.

And make the feature useful before access, if possible. Let people type a city, then offer location as a shortcut.

## Privacy as architecture

Privacy is not only about prompts. It is also about where data goes, which brings back episode one.

Data processed on the device never leaves it. Data processed on a server travels and is stored somewhere. These have very different privacy properties, and users increasingly care.

This is why on device processing has become such an important architectural choice, especially for AI. Episode eight covers this in depth. For now, notice that privacy is a property of the system design, decided by where state lives and where processing happens, not something added at the end with a policy page.

## Worked example one: a smart album feature

An assistant offers to create albums of your best moments from each trip. It needs photos and location metadata.

Granted: albums appear automatically, with places named.

Partial photo access: albums are built only from selected photos. The feature says so: based on the photos you have shared with this app.

No location: albums are grouped by date instead of place. Still useful.

Denied photos: the feature explains what it does in one line, shows a sample, and offers to ask again from settings.

Four states, each designed. None of them is an error screen.

## Worked example two: where permissions and AI collide

An AI assistant inside an app can read the user's messages to help draft replies. The user granted access for that purpose.

Months later, the team adds a feature: the assistant summarizes your relationships and suggests who to reconnect with. Same data. Same permission. Very different use.

Technically allowed. But many users would feel betrayed. They agreed to reply help, not relationship analysis.

This is a key principle in privacy design, sometimes called purpose limitation: data granted for one purpose should not quietly be used for another. AI makes it easy to extract new meaning from old data, so this principle matters more each year. A good spec states the purpose of each data access, and new purposes get a new, explicit ask.

## Going deeper: agents and delegated access

Agents raise a new identity question. When an agent acts for you, whose identity does it use?

If an assistant books a table, it may act with your account and your name. If it reads your calendar, it uses your permissions. The system must know that the agent is acting on your behalf, and the people on the other side may want to know that too.

The design principles from earlier seasons apply. Make delegated actions visible to the user. Use the impact grid to decide what the agent may do alone. And consider disclosure, the diligence part of the 4D framework: should the restaurant know a request came from an agent?

There is not yet one industry standard for all of this. But designing with these questions in mind will age well.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against granular permissions: they create friction. Every prompt, every partial state, every explanation slows people down. Many users just tap allow to get on with it. So all this care may protect very few people.

There is truth here. But the response is that permissions are not only for the careful few. They shape trust across all users, and they are increasingly required by platforms and law. And good design reduces friction: asking in context, at the right moment, with clear value, gets more genuine yes answers than asking everything at launch.

## Practice scenario

Your team designs an assistant that can suggest replies using context from your calendar and messages.

Your question. What permissions does it need, when should it ask, and what does it do with partial or denied access? Which future use of the same data would need a new ask?

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Separate account level and device level for every setting and piece of data.

Two. Design every access dependent feature for granted, partial, and denied. Ask in context, with clear value.

Three. Privacy is architecture: where processing happens, and what purpose each access serves. AI raises the stakes.
