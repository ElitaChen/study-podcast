# X4: Writing Decisions Down

About 10 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, the kernel of strategy and the crux. Strategy sets direction. But most of a designer's daily influence happens in smaller moments: individual decisions about how a feature should behave.

Today, the tool for those. The decision record.

## The bottom line

A good decision record states one question, the context, the criteria that matter, the real options with their trade offs, the decision, and its consequences. It makes trade offs visible, lets the right person decide quickly, and leaves a record of why. Engineering teams have used this format for years, under the name architecture decision record. Design teams can borrow it almost directly.

## Why write decisions down

Many design decisions are made in meetings and then forgotten. Months later, someone asks why the assistant never shows destructive actions in cards. Nobody remembers. The debate starts again from zero, sometimes reversing a good decision for a bad reason.

A short written decision fixes this. It also does something important for you: it makes judgment visible. Tacit design knowledge becomes explicit. For a designer whose value is increasingly in decisions rather than pixels, a trail of clear decision records is evidence of strategic contribution.

## Borrowing from engineering

Software architects have a well established habit: the architecture decision record, or ADR. The technology consultancy Thoughtworks has recommended lightweight ADRs for years. The basic idea: one short document per significant decision, stored where the team can find it.

The core sections, across most templates: context, options considered, decision, and consequences. Many teams add status, such as proposed, accepted, or superseded, so readers know whether a decision still stands.

A rule of thumb from ADR practice translates perfectly to design: if the person changing this decision in six months would need to know the original constraints and the rejected options, write it down.

## The structure for design decisions

One. Title: the decision as a short question. Should generated cards include destructive actions?

Two. Status: proposed, accepted, or superseded by a later decision.

Three. Context: two or three sentences. Why this needs deciding now, and what constraints apply.

Four. Criteria: what matters in this decision. User trust. Task speed. Engineering cost. Consistency across platforms. Keep it to a few.

Five. Options: usually two to four real ones. Include doing nothing if it is truly possible.

Six. Comparison: how each option does on each criterion. Short and honest.

Seven. Decision: which option, and the main reason. Answer first, from X1.

Eight. Consequences: what changes, including costs and new responsibilities. Not only benefits.

Nine. What would change our mind: one line. This invites useful challenge and tells future readers when to revisit.

## Options are the heart

ADR practitioners say this sharply: a record without alternatives considered is just an announcement. The value is in understanding why other options lost. Without that, a future reader cannot tell whether circumstances have changed enough to revisit.

And write options fairly. The most common mistake is writing weak alternatives to make your choice look good. People notice, and trust drops. Write each option as its strongest supporter would. If a colleague favors another option, ask them to check its description. A fair record is more persuasive than a one sided one.

Every option should have a weakness. If one has none, you have not looked hard enough.

## Worked example one: destructive actions in cards

Title: should generated cards include destructive actions?

Context: the assistant now generates cards. Some tasks, like canceling a reservation, could be done directly in a card. A launch is planned next quarter.

Criteria: user trust, task speed, consistency across platforms.

Option A: never. Destructive actions always go to the standard interface. Safest, simplest rule. Slower for some tasks, and users may find the handoff jarring.

Option B: allowed, always with a structured confirmation. Fastest. Needs rules, checks and careful copy, and confirmation fatigue is a risk.

Option C: allowed only for actions that can be undone. A middle path. Needs a clear, reliable undo for each action, which is engineering work.

Decision: C, because it keeps speed where mistakes are cheap, and protects trust where they are not.

Consequences: engineering must build undo for every action exposed in cards. Actions without undo stay in the standard interface. The spec gains a new rule.

What would change our mind: evidence that users miss the undo in testing.

## Worked example two: where decision records fail

A team adopts decision records enthusiastically. After three months, there are four records, all from the first two weeks. Nobody writes them anymore.

ADR practitioners call this the graveyard: a folder created once and never added to. Why does it happen? Usually because the format was too heavy, or because records were seen as paperwork after the decision, instead of a tool for making it.

Fixes from practice. Keep them to one page, often less. Write the record before the decision meeting, as the agenda, not after it. Store them where people already work, next to the specs. And reference them: when a question comes up, link the record instead of re-explaining. Once people see records saving them time, the habit holds.

## Scale the effort to the decision

Not every decision deserves a record. Use the grid from P3. If a decision is easy to reverse and low impact, a line in the spec is enough. If it is hard to reverse or high impact, write the record. Most teams that succeed with this write a handful per quarter, not dozens.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against decision records: they slow teams down. Writing takes time, and fast moving teams prefer to decide in conversation and move on. Some argue that the code, or the shipped design, is the real record.

The response: a shipped design shows what was chosen, never why, and never what was rejected. That why is exactly what gets lost, and exactly what a future team needs. A one page record takes perhaps thirty minutes. Reopening a forgotten debate takes far longer.

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Write important design decisions down, briefly: context, criteria, options, decision, consequences.

Two. Options are the heart. Write them fairly, each with a weakness.

Three. Keep records light, write them before the meeting, and link them often, or they become a graveyard.
