# R4: The 4D Framework, From Personal Skill to Design Check

About 10 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, retrieval failures, and why content design is retrieval design.

Today we take a framework you met in Anthropic's AI Fluency course and go deeper than the course does. First, what the framework really contains, including parts that are often skipped. Then, how to turn it from a personal habit into a tool for reviewing AI features. And finally, its limits.

## The bottom line

The four Ds are Delegation, Description, Discernment, and Diligence. The course teaches them as skills for people working with AI. Read from the product side, they become four questions every AI feature has to help its users answer. The framework is useful, but it was designed for individuals, and it gets blurry once agents work toward goals instead of following prompts.

## Where the framework comes from

The 4D framework was developed by Anthropic together with two professors, Rick Dakan of Ringling College of Art and Design, and Joseph Feller of University College Cork. In August 2026, Anthropic made it the core of the newly launched Claude Academy, and said it is taught to new employees on their first day, as part of what the company calls ever boarding: learning that never formally ends, because AI capabilities keep changing.

## The four Ds, with their sub skills

Most summaries stop at four words. The course actually breaks most of them down further, and the breakdown is where the depth is.

Delegation. Deciding what work to hand to AI and what to keep. It rests on two kinds of awareness. Problem awareness: understanding your goals and what the task really needs. And platform awareness: understanding what the AI system is good and bad at.

Description. Explaining what you want. The course splits this into three parts. Product: what the output should be. Process: how the AI should approach the task. And performance: how it should behave while working with you, its tone and style of interaction.

Discernment. Judging what comes back. It mirrors description, with the same three parts. Is the product good? Was the process sound? Was the performance appropriate?

Diligence. Taking responsibility. Choosing the right AI for the job, being transparent about using it, and owning the final result when you deploy or share it.

Take a moment. Name the three parts that description and discernment share, and why it makes sense that they mirror each other.

## Why the mirror matters

That mirror is the most useful idea in the framework. What you describe is what you should later check. If you described the product but not the process, you can only judge the product. If you never described the expected tone, you have no standard for discernment on tone.

In design terms: the spec and the review use the same criteria. This is exactly the link between P1, where the spec states intent, and P5, where examples and checks verify it.

## From personal skill to product question

Now the turn. If users need these four skills to use AI well, then a good AI product should help them with each one. Each D becomes a product question.

Delegation becomes: does the product make clear what this feature can and cannot do? Can users tell when to hand off, and when to do it themselves? This is platform awareness, built into the interface.

Description becomes: does the product help users say what they mean, about the product, the process, and the performance? Good defaults, examples, clarifying questions, structured inputs.

Discernment becomes: does the product help users judge the result? Sources, honest uncertainty, easy comparison, easy checking.

Diligence becomes: does the product support responsibility? Confirmation before high impact actions, clear records, undo, and honest disclosure that AI was involved.

## Running a 4D review

Here is a practical method. Take any AI feature. For each D, ask its product question, and rate it: strong, weak, or missing. Then focus your next design effort on the weakest D.

A common pattern: most features are strong on description, because teams spend a lot of effort on input. Many are weak on discernment and diligence, because those happen after the output, where design attention often stops.

## Worked example one: a reply drafting feature

An assistant drafts replies to messages.

Delegation: does the user know it only drafts and never sends? Strong, if a clear label says so, and the send button stays with the user.

Description: can the user say make it shorter, or more formal? Strong for product. But can they shape the process, like ask it to check my calendar before suggesting a time? Weak. And performance, its tone toward the user, is not adjustable. Missing.

Discernment: can the user see what the draft is based on? Often weak. A small line, replying to the question about Thursday, would help a lot.

Diligence: nothing sends without a tap. Strong. But is it visible later, to the user or the recipient, that a reply was AI drafted? Missing.

Weakest Ds: discernment and diligence. That is where the next design work goes.

## Worked example two: where the framework gets blurry

Now an agent. The user says: get my expense report done for this month.

The agent searches receipts, categorizes them, fills a form, and asks before submitting.

Where is delegation, and where is description? The user delegated a goal, not a task. The agent then decided the steps, which is a kind of description the agent did to itself. The line between the two Ds blurs.

And discernment changes too. The user cannot judge every intermediate step. They can only judge the final form, and maybe a summary of what the agent did.

This is a real limit. One analysis of the framework, by the research group Envisioning, notes exactly this: the boundary between description and delegation blurs when an agent is given a goal rather than a prompt.

For design, it means agent features need extra support for discernment: a clear record of steps, highlighted assumptions, and a review moment before anything final happens. That is the P2 and P3 work again.

## The strongest counterargument

The same analysis raises a broader critique. The framework focuses on individual competence. It says little about organizations or systems: governance, audits, policies, shared responsibility across a team. It complements those, but does not replace them.

For your purposes, this critique is actually helpful. It marks where the 4D review ends and where other tools take over. For system level questions, like what an assistant may do across a whole platform, you need the spec, the semantic layer, and decisions at team level, not only a user skill frame.

## Practice scenario

Pick an AI feature from your own product, or one you use daily.

Your question. Run the four Ds. For each one, strong, weak, or missing? Then: which single design change would lift the weakest D the most?

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. The four Ds have sub skills. Description and discernment mirror each other: what you describe is what you check.

Two. Turned into product questions, the four Ds make a fast review. Most features are weakest after the output.

Three. The framework blurs with agents and stops at the individual level. Use it alongside specs and team level decisions.
