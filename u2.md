# U2: Measuring Trust

About 11 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, four research methods for AI features, and the debate about simulated outputs. Today, the quality this whole series keeps coming back to. Trust. How do you actually measure it?

## The bottom line

The goal is not maximum trust. It is calibrated trust: people rely on the system as much as it deserves, no more and no less. To measure it, you need two kinds of evidence together, what people say and what people do, and you need to compare both with how accurate the system really is. Self reported trust is consistently higher than behavioral trust, so surveys alone will mislead you.

## Calibrated trust

Too little trust: people check everything, redo the work, and the feature saves no time. It delivers no value.

Too much trust: people accept everything, miss errors, and get hurt when the system is wrong. Errors flow straight through.

Good design aims for the middle. High trust where the system is reliable. Low trust where it is not. A 2026 practical framework put it with a clear example: a user who trusts a sixty percent accurate AI at a ninety percent level is over trusting and at risk. A user who trusts a ninety five percent accurate AI at forty percent is under trusting and missing the value.

So the question is never simply: do users trust it? It is: does their trust match the system's real accuracy, task by task?

## What people say

Questionnaires can capture stated trust. Validated trust scales exist in academic research, and short versions are practical for product teams. Typical items ask how much people agree with statements like: I can rely on this. I understand why it gave this result. I would notice if it made a mistake.

The practical advice from several 2026 guides: academic precision matters less than consistency. Use the same few items every time, so you can see trust change across releases.

But there is a known problem. Self reported trust tends to run higher than what behavior shows. People say they check the outputs. They often do not. One 2026 guide on AI research made this its central point: a single rating question does not measure trust, because what people report and how they act diverge, especially with AI.

Take a moment. In your own words, why might people report more careful behavior than they actually show?

## What people do

Behavior is usually more honest.

Verification: do people open sources, or the original, before acting?

Time before action: how long between seeing the AI output and acting on it? Very fast action on complex output can signal over trust.

Edits: from M1. Rising acceptance without edits may mean growing trust, or growing complacency. You need accuracy data to tell which.

Recovery after failure: after a wrong result, do people keep using the feature, stop, or start checking everything?

Override: how often do people choose the manual path instead?

## Comparing trust with accuracy

Here is the key move. Put trust signals next to real accuracy, for each kind of task.

If the system is right ninety five percent of the time, and people check every output, trust is too low. Design can build confidence: show sources, show a track record, reduce the friction of checking.

If the system is right seventy percent of the time, and people accept without checking, trust is too high. Design should add friction: clearer uncertainty, confirmations, side by side comparison.

One feature may need both, for different tasks. An email summarizer may be excellent on facts and weak on who agreed to what. Trust should be high for the first and low for the second.

## The signature of over reliance

A 2026 paper on evaluating AI assisted home search described a useful pattern. When people rely heavily on AI recommendations, but later outcomes are poor, for example viewings that do not turn into offers, or low ratings after visits, that combination is the behavioral signature of over reliance.

The general form: high reliance now, plus poor outcomes later. You need both signals, across time, to see it.

## A failure test

A practical study design. Let people use the feature normally for a while. Then show one clear failure. Then measure what happens next.

Calibrated users become more careful for a while, then return to normal use as the system proves itself again.

Over trusting users never notice the failure at all.

Over reacting users stop using the feature entirely, even where it is reliable.

The distribution of these three groups tells you whether your trust design, from B5, is working.

## Worked example one: an email thread summarizer

Accuracy testing shows the summarizer is very reliable on facts, but sometimes misses who agreed to what.

Research shows people trust it fully, and rarely open the threads. Over trust, exactly on the weak point.

Design response: when a summary includes a commitment, link it directly to the message where it was made. People can check the risky part in one tap, and trust the rest. A month later, behavioral data shows verification rising for commitments, and staying low for facts. That is calibration in action.

## Worked example two: where trust measurement misleads

A team runs a survey after launch. Trust scores are high. They celebrate.

Three months later, a serious error reaches customers, because nobody was checking.

What went wrong? High stated trust was read as success. But trust was never compared with accuracy, and behavior was never measured. The survey captured confidence, not calibration.

A 2026 framework suggests a simple rule here: when trust scores are high, check for over trust by comparing them with behavioral data. A high score is a question to investigate, not an answer.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against calibration as the goal: it assumes users can and should do the work of checking. Many people simply will not. Busy users want a system they can trust fully, and asking them to verify is shifting the burden to them.

That is fair, and it points to a design principle. Calibration should be made cheap. Make verification one tap, only where it matters, and let the system carry the rest. The goal is not users who check everything. It is users who check the right things, easily.

## Practice scenario

Your team's assistant suggests calendar times for meetings. Accuracy is high for simple cases, weaker when several time zones are involved. A survey shows high trust.

Your question. How would you check whether trust is calibrated? What behavioral signals would you collect, and what design change might you make for the time zone cases?

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Aim for calibrated trust, not maximum trust.

Two. Measure what people say and what they do, over time, and expect them to differ.

Three. Compare trust with accuracy task by task, and make verification cheap where it matters.
