# X2: Framing a Problem So Others Act

About 11 minutes.

## Where we left off

Last time, the pyramid principle. Answer first, and its two limits: controversial answers and co design.

Today, the tool Minto designed to deal with the first limit. Before a recommendation can land, the problem has to be framed so that others recognize it as real and worth solving. Get the frame right, and the answer feels like a relief. Get it wrong, and even a good answer feels like your personal preference.

## The bottom line

A good problem frame has three parts. The situation: something true that the listener already accepts. The complication: what changed, or what hurts. And the question that the complication naturally raises. Only then comes your answer. When this works, the listener arrives at the question on their own, and wants your answer before you give it.

## A detail most people get wrong

You may have heard this called SCQA: situation, complication, question, answer. That is how it often appears in articles and courses.

But Minto's own materials call it the SCQ framework. In her original design, the answer is not part of the introduction at all. The introduction is situation, complication, question. The answer is the top of the pyramid that follows.

Why does this matter? Because it clarifies the job of the introduction. Its job is not to persuade. Its job is to create a shared question. Persuasion happens in the pyramid.

## Situation: nothing surprising

The situation is something the listener already agrees with. No debate. No news.

Our team ships AI features every quarter.

This feels almost too obvious to say. That is the point. It puts the listener in a state of agreement, and establishes the context you are both standing in. If your situation contains something surprising or debatable, the listener starts arguing with you in the first sentence.

## Complication: what changed

The complication is what disturbs the situation. Something changed, something is going wrong, or something new is possible.

Engineers rebuild behavior the spec did not describe, and we had three state bugs last release.

The complication creates tension. It is the reason the conversation exists.

## Question: the one it raises

The question is what a reasonable person would ask after hearing the complication.

How do we specify AI features so behavior is decided before build?

If the situation and complication are good, the question feels inevitable. The listener may even ask it before you do. That is the best possible moment to answer.

Take a moment. In your own words, why should the situation contain nothing surprising, and what is the job of the complication?

## Speak in the listener's terms

The same problem can be framed through different complications, depending on who is listening. Your design insight is the substance. Their concern is the doorway.

For a PM: time to ship, scope risk, user outcomes, metrics.

For a tech lead: rework, bugs, ambiguity, maintenance cost.

For leadership: strategy, competition, risk to the brand.

Same underlying problem: AI features behave inconsistently because behavior is not specified. To the PM: we lose two days per feature to rework. To the tech lead: engineers make design decisions under deadline pressure. To leadership: inconsistent AI behavior across our products weakens trust in the platform.

None of these is dishonest. Each is a true consequence. You are choosing which true consequence matters most to this listener.

## Size the complication

A complication without a size is easy to ignore. Add one concrete measure.

Three bugs last release. Two days of rework per feature. Forty percent of users abandon the flow at this step.

You do not need perfect data. One honest number, with its source, changes the conversation. And if you have no number, say what you observed, and how often. In five of eight sessions, users missed the undo. Specific beats impressive.

## Worked example one: proposing a pilot

You want your PM to support a small pilot of an AI readable component system, before a new project enters its definition phase.

Situation: we start the new assistant project next month.

Complication: in the last project, generated screens used patterns outside our design system, and fixing them took about a week of design and engineering time.

Question: how do we make sure generated UI uses our system from the start?

Then the answer, as a pyramid: run a two week pilot with five components written as AI readable contracts. Three reasons: it prevents the rework we saw last time, it is small enough to fit before definition starts, and it gives us a demo to evaluate before committing.

Notice the timing. The complication refers to pain the PM personally experienced. They do not need convincing that the problem is real.

## Worked example two: where the frame fails

Now a version that does not work.

Situation: AI is transforming how interfaces are built.

Complication: the industry is moving toward generative UI and semantic layers.

Question: how should we prepare?

What went wrong? The situation is a broad claim, not a shared fact about this team. The complication is a trend, not a pain the listener feels. And the question is so open that any answer fits.

This is the most common failure: framing from your own interests instead of the listener's reality. You are excited about semantic generative UI. Your PM is worried about next month's deadline. A frame built on your excitement will feel abstract to them, no matter how true it is.

The fix: start from something that already hurts them, and connect it to your idea. The excitement can come later, once the idea has proven itself on a real problem.

## When the listener disagrees with the situation

Sometimes you discover, while speaking, that the listener does not accept your situation. That is valuable information. Stop. You have found the real disagreement, and it is earlier than you thought. Resolve that first. Pushing on to your answer will not work, because it rests on ground they do not stand on.

## The strongest counterargument

The best case against using SCQ: it can feel like a sales technique. People who know the pattern may feel steered, especially if the framing is obviously chosen to lead them somewhere.

That is a fair concern. The defense is honesty. Use true facts, in the listener's terms, and state the complication plainly, including uncomfortable parts. Framing is manipulation only if it hides something. If it reveals the problem clearly, it is just good communication.

## Connections

This connects to X1, since SCQ is the introduction to the pyramid. To M1 and M2, since metrics give complications their size. And to your strategy of handing a demo to the PM and tech lead one on one: SCQ is how that conversation should open.

## Practice scenario

You notice that your team's AI features each handle confirmation differently. Some ask before everything, some never ask. You want to propose a shared confirmation rule, based on impact and reversibility, from P3.

Your question. Write the situation, complication and question for your PM. Then write a different complication for your tech lead. What measure would you use for each?

## The product implications

Three things to take away.

One. Agree on the problem before proposing a solution. Situation, complication, question.

Two. Frame the complication in the listener's terms, with one honest measure.

Three. Start from their pain, not your excitement. If they reject the situation, stop and resolve that first.
